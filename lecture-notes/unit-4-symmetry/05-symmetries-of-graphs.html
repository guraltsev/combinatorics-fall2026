      18. Symmetries of graphs#

      A graph records which pairs of objects are connected: computers joined by a cable, people who know each other, or cities with a direct flight. When a graph is stored in a computer, its vertices receive names, usually the numbers , and the same network can be stored in many ways depending on which vertex gets which name. This raises two counting questions. Given a graph, in how many ways can it be labeled? And how many essentially different graphs with vertices are there, if two graphs that differ only in the names of their vertices count as the same? Both questions are about a group acting on a set: the permutations in rename the vertices and so move one labeled graph to another. The orbit–stabilizer theorem answers the first question, and Burnside’s lemma and Pólya’s theorem answer the second, using the results of Lecture Note 16 and Lecture Note 17.

      After studying these notes, you will be able to do the following.

      • Describe a labeled graph as a set of pairs of vertices and as a coloring of the pairs.

      • Rename the vertices of a graph by a permutation and decide whether two labeled graphs are isomorphic.

      • Find the automorphisms of a small graph and count its labeled copies with the orbit–stabilizer theorem.

      • Find the cycle type of the permutation of pairs induced by a permutation of the vertices.

      • Count graphs on four vertices up to isomorphism, sorted by number of edges, with Pólya’s theorem.
